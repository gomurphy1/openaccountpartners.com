Short-term export credit insurance, explained plainly.

A policy that pays you when a foreign buyer does not. Here is what it covers, what it costs, and how we will place it.

What the policy covers

Export credit insurance protects the invoices you issue to foreign buyers. It responds to two kinds of loss.

Commercial risk

The buyer goes insolvent, or simply fails to pay within a set period after the due date. This is the everyday risk of selling on terms, and the one small exporters most often absorb on their own.

Political risk

The buyer is willing and able to pay, but cannot: currency transfer restrictions, cancellation of an import license, war or civil disturbance, expropriation. Rarer, but when it happens it tends to hit every receivable in that country at once.

What gets paid

Most of the invoice value. Policies carry a coverage percentage, with the exporter retaining a small share so both sides stay careful about who gets credit. We will spell out the percentage, waiting period and any per-buyer limits in the quote.

What it does not cover

Disputes about the goods themselves. If the buyer refuses to pay because the shipment was late, short or defective, that is a contract dispute, not a credit loss. Pre-shipment costs and sales already past due at the time the policy starts are also outside it.

What it will cost

Less than most exporters assume, and usually far less than the discount they already give up to get paid faster.

Short-term export credit premiums are typically a fraction of one percent of insured sales, charged on what you actually ship each month rather than on a fixed annual amount. The rate depends on the buyer's country, the length of terms, the type of buyer and the structure of the policy.

For a small exporter shipping a few million dollars a year on terms, the annual premium is often comparable to a single lost invoice. Many clients find the policy pays for itself the first time a lender advances against an insured receivable that it previously excluded.

We will quote a specific rate after the first conversation. There is no fee for the quote and no fee for our work; like other insurance brokers, we are compensated by the insurer when a policy is placed.

Where we will place it

Our primary market will be the Export-Import Bank of the United States, the country's official export credit agency. Its short-term insurance programs are designed for small businesses, accept exporters that private insurers often consider too small, and are backed by the full faith and credit of the United States.

Eligibility turns mainly on US content: the goods must be substantially made in the United States, and the exporter must be a US company. We will confirm both before we start an application, so no one spends time on a policy that cannot be written.

Where a private insurer is a better fit for a particular exporter, we will say so and help you get there. Our job is to find the right coverage, not to sell one product.

What servicing will look like

A policy is only as good as the reporting behind it. This is the part the big brokers find uneconomic at our clients' size, and the part we are building the firm around.

  1. Buyer approvals

    Before you ship on terms to a new foreign buyer, that buyer needs a credit limit under the policy. We will gather what the insurer needs and get the limit in place, usually within days.

  2. Monthly shipment reports

    Each month, insured shipments are reported and premium is calculated on them. We will prepare the report from your invoice data so the only thing you review is the total.

  3. Overdue notices and claims

    If a buyer runs late, the policy has deadlines for notifying the insurer and filing a claim. We will track those dates and assemble the claim file. You should never lose coverage because a form was late.

  4. Renewal

    Each year we will review your buyer list, terms and volume against the policy and renew or restructure it. Most exporters who insure once keep insuring; the coverage becomes part of how they sell.
